HONEYWELL AEROSPACE INC. (HONA) — Quick Background

Sector / Industry: Industrials / Aerospace & Defense Market Cap: ~$50.9B | Index: spun from an S&P 500 constituent; index membership not independently verified here Closest comps: covered — ARXS, MDA, HAWK; uncovered but the real peer set — HWM, TDG, GE Aerospace, RTX M&A FLAG: Yes — the company itself is the event. Separated from Honeywell International (now Honeywell Technologies, Nasdaq: HON) on 2026-06-29 in a tax-free pro-rata distribution: one Aerospace share for every two Honeywell shares held at the 06-15 record date, 316,939,750 shares distributed. The separation loaded $15.8B of new debt, of which $15.1B was transferred up to the parent. No other acquisitions or divestitures in the last 12 months.

1. Business Description

We make the systems that fly, guide and power an aircraft, and then we spend the next thirty years maintaining them. Our products are avionics and flight-deck software, auxiliary power units, propulsion engines, wheels and brakes, and satellite, navigation and guidance hardware. Our customers are the airframers — Airbus, Boeing, the business-jet OEMs — plus airlines, MRO shops, defence primes and governments. We employ more than 36,000 people out of Phoenix and serve over 10,000 customers.

The economics are not in the aircraft; they are in the installed base. In the second quarter of 2026 we did $4.52 billion of sales, and $2.03 billion of that — 45% — was commercial aftermarket: spares, repairs, overhauls and retrofit upgrades sold against equipment already flying. Original equipment was $679 million, just 15%. That gap is the entire business model. We compete hard, sometimes near cost, to get our box selected onto a platform, because selection is what buys us three decades of high-margin aftermarket revenue on every airframe built to that spec. It is why a single win can matter more than a quarter: this year IndiGo selected our avionics and power systems for an order of 810 Airbus A320neo-family aircraft, the largest new-aircraft-selectable equipment win in our history, and it will be earning for us into the 2050s.

This also explains why the number we point at is backlog: $18.2 billion at quarter end, up 9% year over year, with trailing-twelve-month orders up 8%. Demand is not our problem.

We run three end markets, and they behave differently. Commercial aftermarket ($2.0bn in Q2, +8%) tracks flight hours, and business-aviation hours have been strong. Defense and Space ($1.8bn, +3%) tracks government budgets and is currently our best order book, though the quarter was held back by lower international volumes and the wind-down of a restricted government programme. Commercial original equipment ($0.7bn, +6%) tracks OEM build rates, which recoupled to customer schedules this quarter.

Roughly 56% of what we sell is product and 44% is services, and about half of all revenue is recognised at a point in time — a mix that makes us more of a shipper of hardware than a software company, whatever the avionics label suggests.

What genuinely differentiates us is position rather than technology: we are the incumbent on a very large installed fleet, and an incumbent avionics or APU supplier is not displaced mid-life because requalification costs more than the part. That is a real moat, and it is also why our current problem is not competitive.

Our problem is that we cannot build enough. Management has said so plainly. We are qualifying over 50 new suppliers with 50 more expected in the second half, raising supplier tooling spend 20% half-on-half and doubling it from 2025 to 2027 — roughly 70% of it into castings — and expanding multi-sourced parts by more than 15% this year.

3. Investment Debate

Bull case - The aftermarket annuity is real and it is 45% of revenue. A $18.2B backlog (+9%) and TTM orders +8% mean the constraint is output, not demand — a materially better problem than the reverse, and one that supplier capex can eventually solve. - $15B of new lifetime-value wins year to date, headlined by IndiGo's selection across 810 A320neo aircraft. Selection wins compound: each one seeds thirty years of high-margin spares on a fleet that has not been built yet. - Defence and Space at $1.8B a quarter is the strongest part of the order book into a rising US and European budget cycle, and it diversifies the commercial-cycle exposure that historically defined this asset.

Bear case - Management cut guidance five weeks after separating. Organic growth went 7–9% to 4–5% and pro forma adjusted EBIT went from 7–10% growth to flat-to-3% — roughly $300M of EBIT removed from the year at the first opportunity to set standalone expectations. That is either conservatism being banked or a business that does not yet know its own run-rate. - Margin compression predates the spin and has not stopped: EBIT margin 25.8% → 22.7% → 18.7% across 2023–2025 on rising revenue. Three consecutive years of negative operating leverage in an aerospace upcycle is the single hardest fact here to explain away. - $16.1B of debt against $1.06B of cash, taken on to fund a $15.1B payment to the former parent. Interest is running ~$800M a year against zero before. On guided adjusted EBIT of $4.4B that is servicable, but it converts a formerly cash-rich segment into a levered standalone with far less room for a demand air-pocket. - Supply chain is self-declared as the binding constraint, and the fixes named — qualifying 100 new suppliers, doubling tooling spend, 70% into castings — are multi-year. Castings are the industry's most chronic bottleneck and are not solved by one company's capex.

Key swing factor: whether casting and supplier capacity recovers fast enough to convert the $18.2B backlog into shipments before the market stops crediting demand it cannot see delivered — this is an output story, not a demand story.

4. What to Watch

  • Q3 2026 results, ~late October / early November — the first genuinely clean standalone quarter and the first real test of the 4–5% organic guide. A second cut would reframe the June guidance as a pattern rather than a reset.
  • Supplier tooling and output inflection, H2 2026 — spend is up 20% half-on-half with ~70% going to castings. Evidence of output growth outpacing the 4–5% guide is the bull trigger; its absence is the bear one.
  • The 2030 targets from the June 3 Investor Day — management has explicitly recommitted to them while cutting 2026. Those two statements are in tension and one of them will move.
  • Deleveraging against the 2H free cash flow guide of $1.0–1.5B — maintained even as EBIT was cut, so it is now the guidance line carrying the most weight.

5. Investor Positioning & Sentiment

  • The shareholder base is still rotating and that is the dominant technical. Shares were distributed mechanically 1-for-2 to Honeywell holders on 06-29 rather than sold to buyers who chose the asset. Spin-offs of this shape reliably see forced selling from index and mandate-constrained holders in the first two quarters, and the stock is $160.75 against a $164.01 prior close — soft, but nothing here isolates the technical from the fundamental.
  • Sentiment got a poor opening hand. A guidance cut at the first print is the worst available first impression for a newly independent management team, and it lands against a positive operating backdrop — backlog and orders both up high single digits.
